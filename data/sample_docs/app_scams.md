# Authorized Push Payment Scams

## What an APP scam is

An authorized push payment (APP) scam happens when a victim is tricked
into willingly sending money from their own bank account to an account
controlled by a criminal. Because the customer personally authorizes the
transfer, the payment looks legitimate to the bank's systems: the correct
credentials were used, the device was familiar, and no account takeover
took place. The deception happens before the payment, not during it.

## How criminals run the scam

Most APP scams start with impersonation. Criminals pose as bank staff,
police officers, HMRC or tax officials, solicitors, or investment
advisers, often using spoofed phone numbers and copied logos so the
contact looks genuine. Victims are told their account is under attack
and must move money to a "safe account," or that they owe an urgent fee,
or that a time-limited investment will disappear unless they pay now.
Urgency and secrecy are the pressure tools: the victim is rushed and
warned not to tell anyone, including real bank staff.

Other variants include purchase scams, where goods advertised on social
media never arrive, invoice redirection, where a genuine supplier's bank
details are swapped for the criminal's, and romance scams, where a fake
relationship is built over weeks before money is requested.

## Confirmation of Payee and other defences

Confirmation of Payee checks that the name on the receiving account
matches the name the sender entered, and warns when it does not. It
stops some mistakes and some scams, but criminals coach victims to
ignore the warning, so it is a friction point, not a guarantee.

Banks also monitor for unusual payees, large first-time payments, and
behaviour that suggests coaching, such as a customer staying on the
phone while paying. Customers protect themselves by calling the bank on
a number they trust, never one supplied by the caller, and by treating
any request to move money urgently as a red flag.

## Reimbursement

Rules in several countries now require payment firms to reimburse
eligible APP scam victims, usually split between the sending and
receiving firms, subject to a cap and to exceptions where the customer
ignored clear warnings. Victims should report the payment to their bank
immediately: the faster the report, the better the chance the receiving
bank can freeze what is left.
